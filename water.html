At each water price, each farmer either produces and irrigates the whole farm as needed (buying any water shortfall in the market) or keeps the land fallow and sells their water allocation into the market. We now simulate 470 farmers with land best suited to different crops, with different yields or cost structures. A filled dot is the farmer's seasonal net return per ML. For perennial growers a line runs to an open circle: the price above which they would actually sell, once the cost of losing trees, vines or herds is counted. Within each crop, farmers are stacked from lowest to highest return. Hover over a dot for details.

Why perennials are reluctant sellers. Vines and almond trees take years to re-establish, and dairy herds cannot be rebuilt overnight. Selling water for a season risks destroying the permanent asset, so a perennial grower holds on to water until the price is well above the seasonal return. Switching-cost premiums used here: Dairy +$150–250/ML · Grapes +$200–350/ML · Citrus +$250–400/ML · Almonds +$300–500/ML. Rice and cotton have none.

The Barmah Choke is a narrow stretch of the Murray that limits how much water can be delivered from the upstream (north) part of the basin to the downstream (south) part. Rice and cotton farms sit mostly in the north; grapes, citrus and almonds mostly in the south. If the south wants to buy more water than the choke can carry, the two zones clear at different prices.
Reserve, buyback, or a mix. Set a reserve (taken before farmers receive their allocations, with no payment) and a government buyback (bought from farmers in the market), in any combination. The two graphs show the same market in two ways: all the water in the basin, and only the water that changes hands.
